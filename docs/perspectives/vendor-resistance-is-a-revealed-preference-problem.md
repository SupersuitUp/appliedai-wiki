# Vendor Resistance Is a Revealed-Preference Problem

*What you already use is the real statement of your values. Every device, service, and account you run says you accepted big-tech dependence long ago. The AI stack is where you noticed.*

## Take the inventory first

Before arguing that a practitioner should not depend on a big tech company for their AI work, write down what they already depend on. Call it the **Dependency Inventory**. A typical one:

- Every device is Apple. An iPhone, a MacBook, a Mac mini.
- Amazon, multiple times a week.
- Google Workspace, religiously. Email, calendar, docs, storage.
- Meta social products, sometimes.
- X, sometimes.
- LinkedIn, which is Microsoft.
- GitHub, which is also Microsoft, all day.
- Anthropic, for the AI work.

Eight entries. Seven of them arrived without a debate. The eighth is the one that gets a values conversation.

That asymmetry is what makes the inventory worth writing down. The objection is not being applied to big tech. It is being applied to whatever is new enough that the person still remembers choosing it.

---

## I had the incoherent version myself

When I was at Google I carried a low-grade guilt about working for a big company. I bought into it. Big meant suspect, and being inside meant being complicit in something.

I never followed the thought anywhere. I did not leave, and I did not stop using the products, and I did not name what specifically I thought was wrong or what would have made it right. The guilt cost me nothing and it protected nobody.

That is what most anti-big-tech feeling is at the practitioner level. It feels like holding a principle, and nothing about what the person does ever has to change.

---

## Revealed preference is the harder evidence

Stated preferences are what someone says they value. Revealed preferences are what their behavior shows they value. When the two disagree, the behavior is the more reliable signal, because it was paid for.

A practitioner who has entrusted a decade of email, photos, contacts, calendars, private repositories, and payment history to four companies has already made a revealed-preference statement about big-tech dependence: acceptable, in exchange for products that work. Announcing at the AI layer that dependence is the problem does not overturn that record. It sits on top of it, unreconciled.

The incoherence is not that the person uses big tech. Nearly everyone does. The incoherence is holding a general objection that is enforced in exactly one domain and nowhere else.

---

## The consistent version of the objection is real

There is an honest form of this argument and it is worth naming, because assuming there is not is how a piece like this stops being useful ([Your Reader Already Has the Objection](/perspectives/your-reader-already-has-the-objection)).

Someone could look at the inventory and conclude the answer is to object everywhere, not to stop objecting. That path exists. It means de-Googling, self-hosting mail and files, moving off the app store, running open weights locally, and paying the [portability tax](/concepts/the-portability-tax) in full and on purpose. People do it. It is coherent, it is expensive, and the ones who do it rarely make the argument selectively, because they have already priced it.

There is a second honest form: a specific objection to a specific company for a specific reason, applied to that company. That is a position with content, and it can be evaluated.

Neither of those is the common case. The common case is the general objection deployed once, at the newest vendor, by someone whose other seven dependencies are not under review.

---

## Refusing the better tool is a real cost

Once the incoherence is cleared, what remains is a product decision, and product decisions have a price.

The frontier tools are where the capability lives right now. [Only frontier models replace skilled labor](/perspectives/only-frontier-models-replace-skilled-labor), so the gap between the best available stack and the acceptable-on-principle stack is not a matter of taste. It shows up as work you cannot do, quality you cannot reach, and a slower loop than the person you are competing with.

Paying that price to hold a position you enforce nowhere else in your life is the part worth reconsidering. You are not staying independent. You are staying dependent on Apple, Amazon, Google, and Microsoft, while handicapping the one area where the dependence would have bought you the most.

---

## What is actually required is faith, and it should be earned

Clearing the incoherent argument does not make the concern disappear. It reduces it to the true one, which is smaller and much more useful.

These companies have deep access to your life and your data. Nothing forces them to use it well. Choosing to depend on one is a leap of faith about the people running it and the incentives around them. That is worth saying plainly rather than dressing it up as a technical evaluation.

I have come to know a lot of people at Anthropic, and on that basis I am willing to take the leap for now. "For now" is doing real work in that sentence, and the next section is what makes it affordable to say.

---

## The exit is what makes the faith reasonable

The thing that makes a leap of faith responsible instead of naive is a way back.

My files are mine. Skills, memory, context, artifacts, and prompts live in my own repositories on my own machine, in plain text, under version control ([command centers](/concepts/command-centers), [local-first software](/concepts/local-first-software)). They are not held inside a vendor's servers in a shape only that vendor can read. If a company loses my trust, I move, and what I have built moves with me.

That is the defense that is available to anyone, and it works by structuring the dependence so it can be reversed rather than by refusing it. The vendor gets the inference, the harness, and the deployment ([the harness and the deployment](/concepts/the-harness-and-the-deployment)). You keep the context, the judgment, and the accumulated work ([Your Edge Is Not Your Infrastructure](/perspectives/your-edge-is-not-your-infrastructure)).

Run the inventory. Where the objection turns out to be inconsistent, drop it. Where it turns out to be specific, act on it. Then spend the effort you were spending on the posture on the exit instead, because that is the part that holds up when trust actually breaks.

> **The question is not whether you depend on a big tech company. You already do, in seven places. The question is whether you can leave.**
