# The Evangelist Sees the Frontier Before the Lab Does

*What a model can do is settled in a lab. What it is for is discovered in contact with a specific job, by whoever is standing there. That makes the person deployed into real builds the first to know, and it means an evangelist who is not also an inventor is mis-hired.*

## Two different frontiers

There is a capability frontier and there is an application frontier, and they are not the same edge.

The capability frontier is what the model can do, measured against benchmarks, red teams, and internal evals. The people who train the model own this frontier completely and see it before anyone else, sometimes months before.

The application frontier is what the capability turns out to be *for*: which specific jobs it collapses, which ones it does not touch, and which entirely new shapes of work become possible that nobody had a name for. This frontier is not visible from inside a lab, because it is not a property of the model. It is a property of the collision between the model and a particular domain, and it only exists once the collision happens.

So the strange and true thing is that the people who built the thing do not know what it is for. Not because they lack insight, but because the information is not where they are.

## Where the information actually is

It is in [the last mile](https://outcomegen.wiki/concepts/the-last-mile), which means it is distributed across a thousand domains at once and held by whoever is elbow-deep in each of them.

Discovering that a capability applies to a job requires knowing the job well enough to notice that a thing which was impractical last year is now trivial. That noticing is the event, and it cannot be done by someone who does not know the job, however well they know the model. The advisor's afternoon, the underwriter's checklist, the clinician's intake: what is hard about each of them is not written anywhere, and until someone stands inside it holding a capable model, nobody finds out what the model does to it.

Which means the map of what this technology is for gets drawn from the outside in.

## What follows for the role

If that is where the information is, then the applied-AI evangelist is not a communications function. The role is positioned at the exact place new knowledge is produced, and that changes what it is for and who is good at it.

- **The output is not only adoption. It is intelligence.** Every deployment answers a question the lab could not have answered from inside: what this capability does to a real job, where it broke, what the practitioner refused to accept. That answer is worth as much travelling inward as the advocacy was worth travelling outward.
- **An evangelist who cannot build is at the wrong altitude.** Someone who can only relay what the lab already knows is transmitting old information. The people who find the frontier are the ones who show up and construct the thing with the customer, because the discovery happens during construction and nowhere else.
- **Hire for inventiveness, not fluency.** Product fluency is learnable in weeks. The instinct to look at somebody's work and see a shape nobody has built yet is not, and it is the actual job.
- **The feedback loop is the deliverable.** A field organization that ships adoption and returns nothing upstream has thrown away the more valuable half of what it produced.

## Why this is not the usual devrel argument

The familiar version of this claim is that developer relations should have a voice in product, which is true, mild, and widely ignored.

The stronger version is specific to this technology. Historically, a platform's capabilities were known to its makers and the field's job was to explain them. Here the capability is genuinely general, which means its uses are genuinely unknown, including to the people who made it. Generality is precisely what makes the maker's knowledge incomplete. Nobody at Bell Labs knew what the transistor was for either, and the answer arrived from firms working on problems Bell was not looking at.

That is the correct historical frame. Not a support function with better access, but the part of the organization that touches the unknown.

## What this costs if you get it wrong

An organization that staffs this role as pure advocacy gets a predictable failure. It will be perfectly informed about its own product and systematically late to what its product turned out to be for, because it never held the instrument that measures that.

It also loses the people who would have found out. The person who can walk into an unfamiliar domain and construct something nobody has built is not going to spend long in a role scoped to explaining a roadmap, and they will leave to go build the thing themselves.
