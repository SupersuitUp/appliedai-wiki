# Just-in-Time Context Collection

*Collecting context from a person at the moment their motivation to give it is at its peak, rather than at the moment it is convenient to ask. Willingness is perishable, so the same questions that get ignored in a follow-up email get answered on the spot. In customer-facing settings the same practice goes by a synonym that names its other half: **customer experience-enhancing context collection**, because the collection moment done right makes the experience better rather than taxing it.*

## The failure this names

An operator meets someone worth knowing. The conversation is good, the mutual interest is real, and it ends the way these always end: a handle exchanged, a promise to follow up, a vague intention to get properly acquainted later.

Later, the operator sends the form. It is a good form. It asks reasonable questions and takes four minutes. Almost nobody fills it out.

The instinct is to blame the form. Make it shorter, make it prettier, add an incentive. That diagnosis is wrong, and it is wrong in the same shape as the diagnosis in [Discoverability Is a Just-in-Time Problem](/perspectives/discoverability-is-a-just-in-time-problem): the thing that failed was not the artifact, it was the timing. The person who would have answered those questions gladly no longer exists. What remains is someone who vaguely remembers a good conversation and now has an email asking for homework.

**Willingness decays faster than intent.** They still intend to be in your world. They are simply no longer willing to spend four minutes proving it, because the feeling that made four minutes seem trivial was a property of the moment, not of the relationship.

## Why the moment is worth more than the method

At the peak of the moment, a person is doing several things at once that they will never do again for free:

- They are **holding their own phone**, which is the only device that knows their number, their email, and their camera.
- They are **standing in front of you**, so the ask comes from a person rather than from a notification.
- They **want to be included**, and answering is how inclusion gets expressed.
- They have **no competing context**. Later, the same request arrives beside forty other unread things, none of which are about you.

None of those conditions can be recreated by better copy. This is why the fix is architectural rather than editorial: you do not write a better follow-up, you move the collection to where the willingness already is.

## The ordering rule

Once you accept that willingness is the scarce resource, the design follows from one question asked of every step: **does this step have to happen now, or does it merely feel natural to put here?**

Steps sort into two piles.

**Perishable.** Things that exist only in this moment and cannot be recovered: their phone number, their email, their face, why they were here, who introduced them, what they were excited about tonight.

**Recoverable.** Things they can do any time in the next month with no loss: following an account, joining a group, saving your contact card, reading about what you do.

The rule is that **every perishable step comes before every recoverable one**, and this is almost always the reverse of how these flows get built. The natural authoring order is the narrative order, which starts with the friendly low-commitment asks and works up to the personal ones. That order is comfortable to write and it loses people, because each recoverable step hands the phone to another application, and a meaningful share of people never come back to finish.

A flow that opens a social app before it has captured an email address has spent the moment on the recoverable half.

## What makes it not feel like extraction

Collecting personal information from someone the instant you meet them can read as either flattering or predatory, and the difference is not subtle. This is the half the synonym names: when the ask is proportionate, consented, and reciprocated, the collection moment is itself a better experience for the person, which is why a live audience answers a show's question gladly while the same audience ignores a survey. Three things separate the two readings.

**The ask is proportionate to the moment.** Twenty seconds of typing at the peak of enthusiasm is a fair trade. The same person asked for a taste profile, a birthday, and six recommendations in that same moment would correctly feel farmed. Depth is what the follow-up is for; the moment is for identity.

**Consent is stated where the capture happens.** Not in a policy, not on a page nobody opens, but in one plain sentence on the same screen as the fields: what you will do with this, what you will not do, and how they leave. The moment is exactly when someone is least inclined to read carefully, which is why the sentence has to be short and true rather than thorough.

**Something arrives immediately.** A message that lands before they have left the room is the proof that the exchange was real. It converts an act of trust into a completed transaction, and it does the recoverable steps for you: the links they did not tap are in the message they now have.

## Depth comes later, and it comes differently

The moment buys identity. It does not buy depth, and trying to take both is how you get neither.

Depth is collected in a second pass, sent to people who now have a reason to answer, and it works because the first pass already established that you are real. The second pass can afford to be fun where the first had to be fast: a questionnaire that plays like a card rather than a form, one question per screen, most of them optional.

This is the [corpus and projection](/concepts/the-corpus-and-the-projection) pattern applied to people rather than documents. The moment produces the raw record; every later artifact, a recommendation, an introduction, a map of where people with taste actually go, is a projection of a corpus that only exists because someone captured the perishable half while it was still there. The same discipline drives the [capture-first autobiography](/playbooks/capture-first-autobiography), where the rule is to get the raw truth down first and shape it into forms afterward.

## Where this generalizes

The pattern is not about parties, and the tell that an operator needs it is always the same: **a form with a low completion rate that everybody agreed to fill out.**

- A workshop that collects a roster afterward instead of in the room.
- An onboarding flow that asks about goals in week two, once the excitement of signing up has passed.
- A support interaction that surveys satisfaction the next day, once the relief of being helped has faded.
- A sales conversation that ends in "I'll send you something" rather than capturing the specific thing the buyer just said they cared about.

In each case the same correction applies. Find the moment where the person most wants to be in the relationship, and move the smallest irreducible ask to that moment. Everything else waits, and the [first touch sells only the next step](/perspectives/your-first-touch-should-sell-the-next-step) rather than trying to close the whole relationship at once.
