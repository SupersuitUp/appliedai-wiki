# Create a feedback receipt

Someone spent an hour of their life reviewing your work. What they almost never get back is evidence of what happened next. They find out at the next meeting, or they never find out, and the quiet lesson is that reviewing your work is a donation.

A **feedback receipt** closes that loop: one page, every item they raised, what it looked like before, what it looks like now, and an honest answer for the ones you did not take. It takes minutes to produce from work you already did, and it is the single cheapest thing you can do to make a good reviewer review you again.

## When to reach for this

- A reviewer sent notes on a deck, a draft, a product, a design, or a plan, and you have acted on some of them.
- A user or customer gave you a list of complaints and you shipped against part of it.
- Anyone senior gave you their time and you want them to give it again.

Do not use it to report on feedback you have not acted on yet. A receipt is written after the work, and its whole authority is that every claim in it is already true.

## The rules that make it worth reading

**1. Every item gets an answer, including the ones you declined.** This is the rule the whole artifact rests on. A receipt listing only your wins is a victory lap wearing a thank-you costume, and a good reviewer notices immediately that their two hardest notes are missing. Declining well earns more trust than agreeing easily.

**2. Quote them, do not paraphrase them.** Use their actual words for each item, lightly trimmed. A paraphrase lets you soften the note into something you already did, and the reviewer can tell.

**3. Before and after is the unit.** Every item shows the state you changed FROM and the state you changed TO. That is what makes it a receipt rather than a summary.

**4. Match the medium to the change.** A copy change shows the old text and the new text. A structural change describes the old shape and the new shape. **Only a visual change needs a pair of images.** Two screenshots per item is noise when the change was a sentence, and it buries the items that genuinely need to be seen.

**5. Verify before you claim.** Every "after" must be live and checkable at the moment you send. Open the URL, load the deck, run the thing. A receipt containing one claim that is not yet true poisons the other nine.

**6. Do not inflate the score.** If you took four of seven, the page says four of seven. The reviewer already knows how many notes they wrote.

**7. Send it fast.** The value decays sharply. Same day is a thank-you, a week later is an audit.

## How to decline an item well

Most receipts fail here, so give it real words. A good decline has three parts:

- **What you understood them to be saying**, in their terms, so they know it was received rather than skimmed.
- **Why you did not do it**, in one honest sentence. "This is right for a cold reader and my live version controls pacing" beats "noted, will consider."
- **What you did instead**, when something else addressed the underlying worry. Often their diagnosis is right and their prescription is not, and saying so respects them more than silent compliance.

Never decline by omission, and never decline with "great point" and no change. If a note genuinely changed nothing, say that plainly.

## The shape of the page

One page, scannable in a minute, readable in five.

1. **A real thank-you**, one or two sentences, specific to what their review actually did. Name the single note that changed the most.
2. **The tally**, stated plainly: how many items, how many taken, how many declined, how many still open.
3. **One block per item**, in their numbering, each with: their words, the verdict (taken, declined, or open), the before, and the after.
4. **The open ask**: what you want them to look at next. A receipt that does not ask for the next pass is a closing statement, and the point was to keep the loop running.

Keep the visual language quiet. This is a thank-you note, not a product launch. Their words should be the most prominent thing on the page.

## Publishing and sending

Publish it as a standalone page at a stable URL and send the link with a short personal message. Do not paste the contents into a chat thread; the artifact is doing the work, and a wall of text in a message undoes the courtesy.

The message itself is three lines at most: thanks, here is what your notes did, what should I look at next. Their name, not "hey team."

## Why this is worth building

Two things changed at once. Producing a per-item before-and-after used to mean hours of manual diffing, so it was reserved for formal design reviews and never done for a friend's late-night notes. And a reviewer's honest read is now the scarcest input in a fast build, because generating options is cheap and knowing which one is wrong is not.

So the economics inverted. The receipt costs minutes, and the thing it buys, a person who will read your work carefully again, is the thing you cannot generate.

> A reviewer who never learns what their feedback did is a reviewer you get once.
